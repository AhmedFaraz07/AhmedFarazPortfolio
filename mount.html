Overview

GENERAL

In March 2026, I designed this back wheel mount for the UTSM 2027 prototype vehicle. The project involved reverse-engineering a real-world part to create a base model, which was then modified for optimized performance. A key modification included the addition of two structural slots designed to reduce unnecessary material weight without compromising the part's integrity.

The primary objective was to enhance the vehicle's mass distribution and stress properties, ensuring the mount can withstand the operational loads of the racing environment while contributing to the overall efficiency of the prototype.

ANSYS 2026 R1

Hand-Calculation Cross-Check

VERIFICATION

This was my first static structural model, so before trusting the output I verified it independently. Treating the loaded section as a bar in uniaxial tension, I calculated the expected extension by hand and compared it against the solver.

TABLE 2 — DEFORMATION: HAND CALCULATION vs. SOLVER

The hand calculation lands within ≈18% of the converged FEA deformation — close enough to confirm the model was set up correctly and was not producing garbage from garbage. The hand figure sits slightly low by design: it idealises the loaded region as a prismatic bar and ignores the stress concentration around the hole, both of which the solver captures.

Deformation is the honest quantity to cross-check here. The hand calculation produces a nominal direct stress of σ = F/A = 4.17 MPa, whereas the solver reports the XY shear component at 7.6683 MPa — two different stress measures, so comparing them directly would prove nothing. The displacement comparison is like-for-like, which is why the verification rests on it.

Mesh Refinement & Convergence

SOLUTION QUALITY

A single mesh proves nothing on its own, so the model was refined until the result stopped moving. The mesh was driven to 124,000 nodes — the ceiling of the Ansys student licence — and the deformation result changed by only 4.2% between the initial and refined meshes, indicating the solution had effectively converged.

Structural Optimisation

MASS REDUCTION

With the baseline model verified, I ran an Ansys structural optimisation on the mount to remove material wherever it was not carrying load, constrained so that maximum stress remained at 7.7 MPa — the converged value from the verified baseline. The optimised geometry took the part from 28.76 g to 14.09 g — a 51.0% reduction in mass.

Mass before28.76 g
Mass after14.09 g

For a Supermileage prototype this matters directly: every gram removed from an unsprung structural component is energy the vehicle no longer has to carry, which is the entire premise of the competition.

Post-Optimisation Validation

RE-ANALYSIS

An optimisation result is not a verified part until it has been solved again in its own right, so the optimised geometry was re-meshed and re-run through the same static structural setup. Maximum stress came back at approximately the same value as the verified baseline, confirming that the 51% of material removed was genuinely not carrying load rather than the constraint simply being reported as satisfied.

Assumptions & Limitations

SCOPE OF VALIDITY

Stated plainly, so the results are read for what they are:

  • Linear elastic, isotropic material response — no plasticity or material non-linearity.
  • Static loading only; fatigue, impact, and vibration are outside the scope of this analysis.
  • A single 500 N tensile load case at the top hole — a conservative placeholder pending measured data; combined cornering and braking cases were not analysed.
  • Fastener preload and contact behaviour at the mating faces were not modelled; the slots are idealised as fixed supports and the central bore as a cylindrical support.
  • Hand calculation treats the loaded region as an idealised prismatic bar, which is why it under-predicts the solver.
9

Method & Learning Curve

PROCESS NOTES

The simulation methodology used here — meshing strategy, convergence checking, and validating solver output against independent hand calculations — came from A Hands-on Introduction to Engineering Simulations (CornellX), which I took specifically to make sure I could run and defend an FEA properly rather than simply generating a colourful contour plot.

Being new to SolidWorks, the CAD side was its own learning curve. Moving from technical requirements to a 3D parametric model required a deep dive into sketch relations, extrusions, and hole-callout features, and working through it gave me a practical understanding of design for manufacturing (DFM) and structural optimisation.
